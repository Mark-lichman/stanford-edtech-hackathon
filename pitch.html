Lantern

A book that starts where a kid already loves something, sends her away from the screen, and ends on purpose.

Maya is nine. She reads about a year and a half below her grade, and she knows it, because somebody told her once and it stuck. She will talk about monsters until you leave the room, and she draws them in the back of her maths book. She has a shared tablet and about forty minutes after dinner.

Her first night is below, told in fourteen numbered beats. But first — what the thing is actually made of, because it's five ideas welded together and it helps to know which one you're looking at. Each one lists the beats where it does its work, and the numbers are links.

A book that raises a kid, then gets out of the way

Stolen from a novel — The Diamond Age, where a girl gets a book that knows her name, tells her a story she's a character inside, and sends her on errands in the real world. It teaches her everything, and the whole point is that she outgrows it.

So: no exercises, no quizzes, no lessons. The book only ever tells the story, and the story is the teaching. And it has an ending, announced on night one, because a book that plans to leave has to say so early or the leaving feels like being dropped.

Watch for it at beats 01, 04 and 14.

The voice that tells her she can't

Around nine, most kids start hearing a running commentary on themselves, and for a kid who's behind in reading it says something specific and cruel. We are not trying to fix that. We're trying to make it visible — a character, with a name she gives it, writing in grey pencil in the margin where she can rub it out with a finger.

That's the whole intervention: she notices it spoke. The book asks once, she taps once, and then it lets it go. It never tells her what to think, never scores it, never stores it, and never shows it to a grown-up.

Watch for it at beats 02 and 12 — and fading out across 13.

Make it so small she can't fail

The habit end of it. Hook the thing to something she already does — closing the book — and make the action so small it survives her worst day. One drawing. One thing on one page. Then celebrate in the first second, out loud, in her own voice, because the feeling is what makes it stick and the feeling has about a second to arrive.

Scaled for a nine-year-old that means a smaller action than you'd write for an adult and a louder celebration. And never a list of what she owes us.

Watch for it at beats 05 and 10.

The thing she makes with her hands

Every night ends with a real object, made off the screen, and it is not an activity we invented and bolted onto a book. Hilda's own practice is the project. She goes out, she looks at something for longer than anybody else would, and she draws it and names it. That is what the character does, for six books.

So Maya starts her own field guide. Find one thing in this house nobody has ever really looked at — behind the radiator, the underside of a shelf, the corner of a window. Sit in front of it. Draw it. Give it a name, like it's a creature. Write one true thing about it underneath.

Paper and a pencil, indoors, alone, no tools and nobody else involved — which is the rule every quest in the bank has to pass before it ships. And the page she makes tonight isn't a souvenir of the app. It's page one of a real object that gets thicker for eight weeks.

And once, weeks in, a page turns up in it that she didn't draw. Her guide is hers — it is just not the only one of its kind.

Watch for it at beats 05, 08 and 10 — and the page that isn't hers in 13.

Start where she already is, then widen

She tells us one thing she likes. Everything for the first week is made of that thing. Then one of tonight's three doors is a half-step outside it, and she's the one who picks.

Two rules keep it honest. Home ground is on the board every night, so a hard day has a soft landing. And when it's a bad week the doors quietly move back toward where she started — she's never told, nothing looks different, the book just stops pushing for a while. A comfort zone that only ever widens isn't comfort, it's a ratchet.

Watch for it at beat 03, and the eight-week walk in 13.

And the reading itself happens in Epic

This is the part worth being blunt about. Lantern has no library. It writes roughly eighty words a night — a doorway, a hook, a question she now needs answered — and the actual book she reads is a real book in Epic's catalogue.

Tonight that book is Hilda and the Troll, by Luke Pearson. It is on Epic right now, and so are the five that follow it — which is what makes the eight-week walk in beat 13 a real journey through real books instead of a diagram.

Lantern writes one page. Hilda writes the other forty. And the quest is built out of that book, so she can't do tonight's quest without having read tonight's book. No quiz required to prove it.

We are not competing for reading time. We're the reason a kid who tests a year and a half behind opens a book on a Tuesday night — and comes back tomorrow for the next one.

She reads

A whole book. Not a chapter of one.

She opens Hilda and the Troll in Epic and reads it end to end. Epic clocks it at fifteen to thirty minutes, which is the rarest thing we found: a real book a nine-year-old finishes in one sitting, on a Tuesday, before bed.

This is the reading, and it is deliberately not ours. It happens in Epic's app, in Epic's catalogue, with Epic's 40,000 books — Lantern has no library and never will. Every minute here is a minute Epic keeps.

It's also the only number in this whole design that matters: a book finished, by a kid who doesn't finish books. She has never done that before, and she did it on a school night.

Eight weeks

the shape of the whole thing

Week two, something comes up out of the story and gives her something. Not a badge — a verb. Noticing. From then on every page carries one detail only she can see, and one of those details turns out, three weeks later, to be the key to a door. One ability a week, eight in all, and each one changes what tomorrow's three doors are allowed to ask of her.

The doors walk outward through books that exist: Midnight Giant, then Bird Parade, then Black Hound, then Stone Forest, and in week eight The Mountain King — longer, darker and further from home each time, and not one night of it felt like a stretch. Grumble's handwriting has been getting fainter for a month, and the book has been taking longer and longer to come back — two nights dark, then five — because the story needs more time to happen out there than in here.

And in week three a page arrives that she didn't draw. A map of somebody else's airing cupboard with the shelves the wrong way round, left behind by whoever got there first. There is no name on it, no message, and nothing to reply to: the stories touch and the children don't. It is the one moment that tells a kid who has decided she is the slow one that other people are out there doing this too — without putting a single one of them in front of her to measure herself against.

By then the field guide has forty-odd pages in it. Not one of them is ours — and one of them isn't even hers.

The problem

before the first tap 88 words · ~35s

A nine-year-old who is behind in reading learns two things at once. That she is bad at this. And that the system she is in has already decided it. One of those is a voice in her head and the other is a reading level on a file, and by nine she cannot tell them apart.

We wanted to hand her back her own story without pulling her out of the system she still has to live inside. Same shelf. Same books. She just stops reading them as somebody's assignment.

Never said out loud

Eight sentences that lose the room.

  • Ten seconds is proof of work, never a fluency measurement. No score, no number, shown to nobody.
  • Not "less screen time." Displacement: four screen minutes buying twenty minutes of reading and fifteen off the screen entirely.
  • Never "2 sigma." It didn't replicate, and we aren't a tutor anyway.
  • Never "saboteur." It is clinical vocabulary, it invites the diagnosis question, and the child-facing word is Grumble.
  • Never "we teach her Sage", or reframing, or affirmations. We stopped at noticing on purpose, and the stopping is the strong part.
  • Never "habit-forming" about our own app. The recipe is pointed at reading a book, not at opening Lantern. The habit is the book.
  • Never "social", "community", "feed" or "other kids" about that page. Those words promise a system we did not build and invite the moderation question before we have said the seeded part. It is a page somebody left behind — that is the whole vocabulary.
  • Never a name, an age or a school attached to that page, on stage or on screen. The one we seeded has none, and saying one out loud invents it.

And one that only exists because the pitch now opens on the problem: never frame the system as the villain. Not school, not reading levels, not Epic — Epic is in the room. The tension is that she has no authorship inside it, and we give her authorship without asking her to leave.

The four questions this invites

Is this Positive Intelligence for kids? Are you assessing children?

No. We took one concept — the self-critical voice — and only to the depth of awareness. What we deliberately did not build: no diagnosis, no score, no profile, no emotional-state data leaving the device, nothing shown to a parent, and she can skip every bit of it. He also appears less over eight weeks, not more. A permanent resident would be a permanent fact about herself.

Isn't Tiny Habits just a behaviour loop with a book on top?

The recipe is Fogg's and we use it straight: anchor, tiny behaviour, celebration she says out loud. Two departures. The behaviour is off our screen, so the reinforcement never points back at the app. And the celebration's noun lands on the artifact, not on the child.

So is the Epic integration real?

The titles and the catalogue are. The reader is a mock, because Epic has no public API — the integration we are showing the intent of is a handoff, and the handoff is the product. We write eighty words; their library writes the rest.

Whose page is that? Is there a social layer — who moderates it?

There are two or three artifacts in that pool and we drew them. Nothing a child wrote reaches another child: no accounts, no names, no chat, no profiles, no free text. The stories touch; the children don't. Anything that needed a moderation answer is out of scope by design, not by deadline.

Answer that one in that order — seeded first, then the rule. Leading with the rule sounds like a policy we wrote after somebody got hurt.

All four are short on purpose. The long version is the one that sounds defensive.
